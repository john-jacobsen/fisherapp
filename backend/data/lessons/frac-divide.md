# Dividing Fractions

## Overview

**Dividing by a fraction** asks how many times one fraction fits into another. The key insight is that dividing by $\frac{c}{d}$ is identical to multiplying by $\frac{d}{c}$ — the reciprocal. This is not a trick to memorize; it follows from the definition of division.

## Key Idea

$$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c} = \frac{ad}{bc}$$

Why does flipping work? Division asks "what do I multiply the divisor by to get the dividend?" If you multiply $\frac{c}{d}$ by its reciprocal $\frac{d}{c}$, you get 1. So multiplying by $\frac{d}{c}$ undoes division by $\frac{c}{d}$ — they are the same operation.

The steps are sometimes called **Keep, Change, Flip**: keep the first fraction, change $\div$ to $\times$, flip the second fraction.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: $\dfrac{3}{4} \div \dfrac{2}{5}$**

You want to know how many times $\frac{2}{5}$ fits into $\frac{3}{4}$. Flip the divisor and multiply: the $\frac{2}{5}$ becomes $\frac{5}{2}$.

$$\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{5}{2} = \frac{3 \cdot 5}{4 \cdot 2} = \frac{15}{8}$$

The answer $\frac{15}{8} > 1$ makes sense: $\frac{3}{4}$ is larger than $\frac{2}{5}$, so the divisor fits more than once.

---

**Example 2: $\dfrac{2}{3} \div \dfrac{4}{9}$**

Flip $\frac{4}{9}$ to get $\frac{9}{4}$, then multiply. Notice that cross-cancellation can simplify before you multiply: the 2 in the numerator and the 4 in the denominator share a factor of 2, and the 9 and 3 share a factor of 3.

$$\frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{4} = \frac{\cancel{2}}{\cancel{3}} \cdot \frac{\cancelto{3}{9}}{\cancelto{2}{4}} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Cross-cancelling before multiplying keeps the numbers small and avoids a separate simplification step at the end.

---

**Example 3: $\dfrac{5}{6} \div \dfrac{5}{12}$**

Both fractions share 5 in their numerators. After flipping the divisor to $\frac{12}{5}$, the 5s cancel immediately.

$$\frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{12}{5} = \frac{\cancel{5}}{6} \cdot \frac{12}{\cancel{5}} = \frac{12}{6} = 2$$

The whole-number result makes sense: $\frac{5}{6}$ is exactly twice $\frac{5}{12}$ because 12 is twice 6.

## Common Mistakes

- **Flipping the first fraction instead of the second.** You always flip the divisor — the fraction you are dividing *by*. The dividend (first fraction) stays exactly as written.
- **Forgetting to flip at all and multiplying straight across.** Multiplying $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}$ without flipping gives the wrong answer; the reciprocal step is not optional.
- **Not simplifying the result.** After multiplying across, check whether the result can be reduced. GCF of numerator and denominator should be 1 in the final answer.
